## Prop Joe: revised timing

You have chosen to cut the layer in April. That changes my earlier advice, and I got it wrong first time.

**Do not wrap the layer now. Wrap it in late February or early March.**

I originally said wrap now and leave it on over winter. Thinking about it properly, that means keeping sphagnum moist inside plastic for eight months through the darkest, coolest part of the year, for no gain. If it dries the roots die, if it stays sodden and cold the wound can rot, and it is exactly the sort of eight-month maintenance job that gets forgotten.

Monstera roots in four to six weeks once the plant is actively growing. Light returns here in late February, so a layer wrapped then has roots by April. Same destination, six weeks of moss to look after instead of thirty-two.

**Realistically you will be cutting in late April or May rather than early April**, because you wait until roots are visibly abundant before separating. That costs nothing.

**The repot is a separate job and it should still happen this month.** Burying 20 to 30cm of bare trunk and tucking the aerial roots into compost while it is warm means those roots convert into working feeder roots before winter. By the time you layer him in spring he is a stronger plant to be taking a cutting from. It also fixes the watering and the wobble now rather than in nine months. Doing the two jobs separately is better than doing both at once anyway.

**Put a calendar reminder on for 20 February.**

## The crate of live moss

It arrived as **live moss, not dried sphagnum**, which is better for most of what you need but has to be stored differently. Uses in order of value:

1. **Wrap it round the monstera poles.** Bind a layer of moss around each existing coir pole with fishing line or soft twine. Bare coir dries out fast and aerial roots grip it poorly; moss they go into eagerly. Three monsteras, all with wasted aerial roots, all getting poles. **This is the best thing you can do with it by a distance.**
2. **A moss layer on the monstera soil surfaces.** Keeps the surface humid so the loose aerial roots on Philipos and OG loc root down into it instead of staying corky. Leave a gap around each stem so the base is not sitting wet.
3. **Bulk out Prop Joe's mix.** Hand-torn sphagnum is a legitimate aroid mix component, and it covers the bark shortfall (see below).
4. **February's air layer** is now already paid for.
5. **Keep the rest alive as stock.** Tray, bright indirect light, kept damp. **Rainwater or filtered only, never tap** — the calcium in tap water kills sphagnum off. No feed. Pick out the seedlings germinating in it or they will take over. Then you have a permanent supply.

**Do not put it near the string of hearts.** That plant wants dry and gritty; moss would rot it.

## The monstera pole principle

**For a monstera a pole is not a stick, it is a food source.** Their aerial roots grip damp moss or coir and convert into working feeder roots, the same conversion you are engineering by burying Prop Joe's. So never just tie the stem on and leave the roots dangling: **tuck them into the pole**, and keep it damp by pouring water down it when you water.

Do not try to bend an existing stem around anything. Monstera stems stiffen and snap. Tie in at three heights with soft ties and **guide the new growth**. The shape comes from what grows next.

**Neither Philipos nor OG loc needs a bigger pot.** Both are 25 to 26cm with plenty of root room; their problem is light and support. Check the drainage holes when you lift them, and only reconsider if roots are pouring out.

## String of hearts: worse than I thought

The photo shows the poorest plant in your collection. Strands almost entirely bare with a few tiny leaves, several dried dead sections, and a **white salt crust** on bone-dry compacted soil.

**9cm to 10cm is barely an upsize, and that is deliberate.** Ceropegia are semi-succulent, they store water in the leaves and tubers, and they rot in too much soil. So the white pot is exactly right and nothing bigger.

- **Grittier mix, roughly half perlite or grit.** Not your general blend, which holds far too much water for this plant
- **Cut the dead strands off** entirely
- **Coil the bare strands back onto the soil and pin them down.** Concretely: lay each long wiry strand in loose loops on top of the compost and pin every loop with a U-pin or bent wire so the stem is actually touching soil. At every leaf pair there is a joint, and Ceropegia root from any joint in contact with damp soil. Each rooted joint then pushes new shoots, so one bald string becomes a dozen short leafy ones coming out of the pot
- **Coiling beats taking cuttings here.** The strand stays attached and fed by the parent while it roots, which matters on a plant this depleted — put everything back into rebuilding it rather than starting separate plants. Do check the dead strands you cut off for any firm pea-sized tubers before binning them, though: a tuber can still be alive when the strand looks finished, and pressed onto damp compost it will root and shoot
- **Brightest spot you have.** Bare strands with widely spaced leaves is a light problem, and Ceropegia want more light than any other trailer you own
- **Let it dry out completely between waterings**

**The hoop or trellis is off the shopping list.** It is already in a hanger, so once the plant is actually full the trailing looks after itself. The problem was never the trailing method.

**Three monsteras.** Prop Joe, Philipos, OG loc. They all want the same things: chunky bark-heavy mix, aerial roots into something damp rather than dangling in air, feeding through summer, and a pole tall enough to matter.

## Soil recipes

Coir and peat are both nutritionally near-inert, and coir actively locks up calcium and magnesium. **Any mix you make must have fertiliser added or the plants start hungry.**

**General** (Dracaena, avocado, pothos): 3 parts coir or peat, 1 perlite, 1 orchid bark, plus granular at label rate or 15% worm castings.

**Chunky aroid** (all three monsteras): 2 parts coir or peat, 2 orchid bark, 1 perlite, plus granular or worm castings. Bark-heavy on purpose; Prop Joe's watering problem is compost so fine that water runs straight past it.

**Cuttings**: 2 parts coir, 1 perlite. **No granular**, the salts scorch cut ends. Worm castings are fine, they cannot burn.

**Strelitzia top-up**: 3 parts coir or peat, 1 perlite, a handful of bark.

**Coir or peat?** Coir rewets better, so use it for anything rot-prone (Dracaena, Strelitzia). Peat holds more water, so save it for thirsty aroids. Use the peat up rather than buying more.

## How to cut back and propagate a trailer

**Find the bumps.** Run your finger along a stem. Every few centimetres there is a slight bump or joint where a leaf stalk joins, or a scar where one used to. Often a small brown stub too. **That bump is the only place roots ever grow from.** The smooth stretch between bumps grows nothing.

**Cutting back.** Find where a stem leaves the soil, follow it out, count bumps. Keep three or four, cut on the smooth part about 1cm past the last one you keep. New shoots come from the bumps you leave. Roughly 10 to 15cm remains. Leave a couple of leafy stems near the base if you can; leaves power the regrowth.

**Making new plants.** Lay the cut lengths out all facing the same way and keep them that way. Chop into pieces with two bumps each, cutting 1cm past a bump at both ends. Strip any leaf off the lower bump, keep any on the upper. Bin anything soft or brown. If you lose track of which way is up, the stem thins toward the tip and the leaves get smaller that way.

**Planting.** Poke a hole with a pencil. Lower bump 2 to 3cm under the surface, upper bump and its leaf above. Three or four pieces per hole. **The mistake that wastes the job is burying smooth stem with no bump underneath.**

**Refreshing soil while it is bare.** Tip the pot sideways, ease the plant out, crumble the loose old soil off the outside and top with your fingers. Do not wash it or pull the roots apart. Back in the same pot, fill the gaps with fresh mix, firm gently.

**After.** Damp not soggy, bright light, no direct sun. Three weeks, then tug gently. Resistance means roots.

## Aerial roots, the thing that matters for all three monsteras

- **In air** they stay thin and corky and do nothing
- **In moist substrate** (compost, a damp pole, sphagnum) they branch into proper feeder roots
- **In water** they make thin brittle roots that transplant badly

So: bury them, or tuck them into a damp pole. Never leave them dangling, and never root an air layer in water.

## Prop Joe: the method, for February

Wrap moist sphagnum and plastic around a bump below the crown, using the aerial roots already there as a head start. Roots in four to six weeks. Wait until they are visibly abundant, then cut below them and pot up.

## The pattern behind all of it

Nine of your plants had the same two problems: **not enough light, and no feed since the compost ran out.** Long bare stems, small leaves, everything running away from the pot. Only one plant in the whole collection was genuinely short of root space. Rotating weekly and feeding fortnightly through summer prevents essentially all of it.
