## Feeding order, once the feed is bought

Six weeks of season left. Start at the top.

- **Strelitzia.** Heavy feeder, and now in better light so it can use it
- **Prop Joe.** Feed him before the repot. Never within four to six weeks after root work
- **Philipos.** Feeding is the only intervention he is getting
- **OG loc.** Third monstera, same story as the other two
- **Avocado.** Hungry, but wait for new shoots. Feeding a leafless stem does nothing
- **The three trailers.** Once they have regrown a little, to fuel it
- **Both variegated pothos.** Moderate. Heavy nitrogen pushes them greener
- **Dracaena.** Half strength, occasionally. Salt-sensitive
- **The pine: do not feed.** Soft late growth will not harden before frost

Then stop everything mid-September until March.

## Soil recipes

Coir and peat are both nutritionally near-inert, and coir actively locks up calcium and magnesium. Any mix made here must have fertiliser added or the plant starts hungry.

- **General** (Dracaena, avocado, pothos): 3 parts coir or peat, 1 perlite, 1 orchid bark, plus granular at label rate or 15% worm castings
- **Chunky aroid** (all three monsteras): 2 parts coir or peat, 2 orchid bark, 1 perlite, plus granular or worm castings. Bark-heavy on purpose
- **Cuttings**: 2 parts coir, 1 perlite. No granular, the salts scorch cut ends. Worm castings are fine, they cannot burn
- **Strelitzia top-up**: 3 parts coir or peat, 1 perlite, a handful of bark
- **String of hearts**: roughly half perlite or grit. Not the general blend

Coir rewets better, so use it for anything rot-prone. Peat holds more water, so save it for the thirsty aroids and use it up rather than buying more.

## The crate of live moss

It arrived as live moss rather than dried sphagnum, which is better for most of what is needed here. Uses in order of value:

- **Wrap it round the monstera poles.** Bind a layer around each existing coir pole with fishing line or soft twine. Bare coir dries fast and aerial roots grip it poorly. Moss they go into eagerly. Three monsteras, all with wasted aerial roots. This is the best use of it by a distance
- **A moss layer on the monstera soil surfaces**, so the loose aerial roots on Philipos and OG loc root down into it instead of staying corky. Leave a gap around each stem
- **Bulk out Prop Joe's mix.** Hand-torn moss is a legitimate aroid mix component and it covers the bark shortfall
- **February's air layer** is now already paid for
- **Keep the rest alive as stock.** Tray, bright indirect light, damp. Rainwater or filtered only, never tap, because the calcium in tap water kills it off. No feed. Pick out any seedlings germinating in it

Do not put it near the string of hearts. That plant wants dry and gritty and moss would rot it.

## Aerial roots, the thing that matters for all three monsteras

- **In air** they stay thin and corky and do nothing
- **In moist substrate**, compost or a damp pole or sphagnum, they branch into proper feeder roots
- **In water** they make thin brittle roots that transplant badly

So bury them, or tuck them into a damp pole. Never leave them dangling, and never root an air layer in water.

For a monstera a pole is not a stick, it is a food source. Keep it damp by pouring water down it when you water. Do not try to bend an existing stem around anything, monstera stems stiffen and snap. Tie in at three heights with soft ties and guide the new growth. The shape comes from what grows next.

## The pattern behind all of it

Nine of these plants had the same two problems: **not enough light, and no feed since the compost ran out.** Long bare stems, small leaves, everything running away from the pot. Only one plant in the whole collection was genuinely short of root space.

The pot count fell at every round of evidence: 4 to 9, then 3 to 4, then 2 to 3, then 2, then 1. Every measurement removed a purchase. Hold that line. **The default answer to "should I repot this" in this collection has been no.** The exception, added on 21 August, was the big variegated pothos, and only because the roots were visibly out of the base and running across the floor.

Rotating weekly and feeding fortnightly through summer prevents essentially all of it.
